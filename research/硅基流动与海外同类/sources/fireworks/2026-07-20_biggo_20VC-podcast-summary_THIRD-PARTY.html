Are OpenAI & Anthropic Overvalued? The Open-Source AI Reality with Fireworks AI, Lin Qiao
HostHarry StebbingsGuestLin Qiao
3 months ago01:28:35en

Key Takeaways

Fireworks AI founder Lin Qiao argues that specialized intelligence from private enterprise data is more valuable than generalized frontier models, predicting a 10x drop in token costs and 100x usage growth as open-weight models enable cost control and every company will own its own intelligence.

Summary

In this episode of 20VC, host Harry Stebbings interviews Lin Qiao, founder and CEO of Fireworks, a specialized intelligence platform that helps companies customize and deploy open-source models at massive scale. Qiao, a former Meta and Facebook engineering leader who started Fireworks at age 48, lays out a provocative thesis: the future of AI is not a single AGI but millions of specialized models, each serving a unique business purpose. She argues that every company will eventually "own their own intelligence" just as every company owns its own software stack, and that the open-source ecosystem—not the frontier labs—will enable this shift. The episode covers the economics of token costs (predicted 10x reduction in three years driving 100x usage), the bottlenecks in AI infrastructure (energy, chips, data center deployment), and the strategic rationale behind Fireworks' recent hiring of George Hu (former Salesforce president) to scale its go-to-market operation. Qiao also shares leadership lessons from her interactions with Jensen Huang and explains why her company, currently processing over 40 trillion tokens per day and at roughly $800 million ARR, is still in its "hypergrowth" phase, prioritizing velocity over gross margin optimization.

The Specialized vs. General Intelligence Divide

The core tension of the episode is between the vision of a single AGI that solves everything (championed by Sam Altman, Dario Amodei, and others) and the reality of specialized intelligence that Qiao believes will dominate enterprise adoption. She argues that the majority of the world's data is private—locked inside corporate systems, not on the public internet—and that activating this data requires models tailored to each company's unique domain.

"If you think intelligence is a derivative of data, then majority of the data is actually not used for training a general intelligence model... It will never get shared with anyone else because this is company's proprietary IP."

Qiao references a conversation with Jensen Huang after his GTC keynote, where Huang said: "There's no specialized general company as in every company is built on a special belief of doing things, otherwise there's no reason they should exist." This led Qiao to her deeper point: every company's existence is justified by something unique—forged into its product design, software systems, data, and user interaction—intelligence that cannot be learned by an external model.

She also makes a civilizational argument:

"If our future world is going to be ruled by one standard, a taste dictated by one company, we turn ourself into an army of robots. And that's very depressing to me."

Thus, the frontier labs (Anthropic, OpenAI) are building a "power line" of general intelligence that will enable many specialized "appliances," but they will not replace those appliances.

Open-Source Models as the Engine of Specialization

Fireworks bet early on open-source models—even when they were in their infancy—because of a belief that "openness gave control." Open models give the user full control over weights, customization, and deployment, which is essential for owning one's intelligence.

Both open and closed models have now crossed a quality threshold enabling a vast array of applications. But open models have an additional advantage: they are much easier to tune with small amounts of unique company data. After tuning, the specialized model often outperforms a general-purpose model on the company's specific eval.

Qiao cites DeepSeek as among the top open models but acknowledges national security concerns. She pushes back by saying every model should have company-specific guardrails anyway, and points out that the open ecosystem is robust enough that the US can build its own if China restricts access.

Token Cost Economics: A 10x Reduction Driving 100x Usage

One of the most data-rich sections concerns cost reduction. Qiao predicts a 10x cost reduction in the next three years (by 2029) driving a 100x increase in usage.

She decomposes the cost reduction into four layers:

  1. Task-level efficiency: As model quality improves, solving a task requires fewer tokens (models become more precise).
  2. Model customization: Fine-tuning to a specific problem reduces wasteful token generation.
  3. Platform optimization: Fireworks specializes in optimizing the unit economics of each token through system-level innovations (zero-KLD inference, distributed RL training).
  4. Underlying hardware: Supply chain constraints (GPUs, HBM, energy) will ease over 2–3 years, further compressing costs.

"I can imagine no, 10x cost reduction in the next three years and this 10x cost reduction will drive a 100x usage."

Harry asks about Marc Benioff's statement that Salesforce is spending 3.8% of developer salaries on Claude Code. Qiao uses it as a baseline but argues it could go much higher if tools become dramatically cheaper and better.

Infrastructure Bottlenecks: Chips, Data Centers, and Energy

Qiao references Jensen Huang's "five-layered AI cake": (top) application → model → infrastructure → chips → energy. She notes the current bottleneck is in the physical layer—energy, chips, and even small components like transistors that hold up server manufacturing.

"We're bottlenecked by the lower part of the AI cake in terms of supply chain."

She discusses the difficulty of building data centers in the US versus China, noting that in China infrastructure construction is extremely fast (a highway built in a week), while in the US even electricians are in short supply. Fireworks does not plan to build its own data centers yet, preferring to run across all available chips and data centers, focusing on the platform layer. However, Qiao leaves the door open for later—"it could always be on the table, but the question is timing."

Regarding moves by OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and DeepSeek to build their own chips, Qiao argues that chip efforts only make sense when a workload has stabilized and become large enough to warrant encoding into hardware. She estimates many of these efforts are "pre-JI" (pre-Jensen invention) bets that have become serendipitously useful as AI workloads evolved.

The Cursor Case Study: Model Customization in Practice

Fireworks' most prominent customer case is Cursor, the AI coding assistant. Qiao describes how her CTO, Dima, embedded with Cursor for months to build the reinforcement learning infrastructure for model post-training.

The architecture: RL training splits into two parts—a trainer that tweaks weights and generates new model versions, and an RL rollout that deploys those versions against a synthetic coding environment, collects rewards, and feeds them back. To avoid the need for an expensive 100,000-chip cluster, Fireworks designed a fully distributed system running across 5–6 data center regions globally, using "scatter GPUs." The challenge is syncing model weights quickly enough to keep rewards fresh.

"Cursor is one of the first companies to decide to work with us early on... They were single-digit million dollar ARR when they started working with us. They grew by 100x to 1000x over two years."

This partnership validated that early-stage application companies should focus on product innovation and rely on a specialized platform for inference and training infrastructure.

Hypergrowth Strategy: Growth Over Margin, for Now

Fireworks has roughly $800 million ARR (announced in 2026) and aims to at least double by end of 2026. The company grew from 50 to 200 employees in about a year. Gross margins are in the 30–40% range, typical for infrastructure companies in hypergrowth.

Qiao explicitly states they are not optimizing for margin now:

"During hypergrowth phase you have the choice... you either optimize to margin or you optimize to growth. If our focus is only optimize growth margin, we absolutely can do that. But we are sacrificing the speed of growth."

She expects margins can increase to 70–80% later when they optimize for differentiation, but that time is not now. The trade-off is between overoptimizing margin (which slows growth) and capturing the market.

Harry asks whether they would move down the stack (to data centers) to improve margins. Qiao says they "absolutely are not going to move into application layer," but data centers are possible eventually—though such moves require specialized expertise in construction, power, cooling, and fiber.

Leadership and Culture: Extreme Ownership and Learned Velocity

Qiao shares several leadership insights:

  • Hiring philosophy: She looks for "extreme ownership"—people who automatically claim an end-to-end problem and deliver "no matter what." The strongest indicator is not just competence but the ability to take full accountability. (Harry jokingly says his firm "only hire immigrants"—Qiao doesn't endorse that directly but emphasizes the extreme ownership trait.)
  • George Hu hiring: She met Hu a year before hiring him, when Fireworks was only 50 people, and told him "we're probably too small for you." They started a consulting relationship, and only when growth exploded did they bring him on full-time. She praises Hu's combination of deep experience and "curiosity—he doesn't assume he knows all."
  • Jensen Huang's secret: Qiao describes how Huang replies to emails in one minute and is "everywhere." She initially wondered if he had clones. Now she understands that because leadership is "judgment, not privilege," a leader must have ground-level context to make fast, correct decisions.

"If you do not know what's happening, what works, what doesn't work, what are the gaps, you make the wrong call. In a fast-moving space you just cannot wait."

Cross-Theme Synthesis

The episode closes with Qiao's firm prediction: within three years, every company will own its own intelligence as a "must-have, not optional." The analogy is software—no successful company today uses a fully standardized off-the-shelf stack; each builds its own because it has unique problems. The same will happen with AI.

She also identifies the most underinvested area: AI ROI monitoring and attribution. As AI moves deeper into production, the industry will need discipline in measuring return on spend.

"The token maxing is just a thing in time—but we'll quickly move into ROI maxing."

Unresolved tensions worth tracking:

  • The pace of open-source model development from China (DeepSeek) vs. the US, and whether US policy will spur its own open ecosystem.
  • The tension between "owning intelligence" (customized models) and the economics of renting from frontier labs, which could become cheaper at scale.
  • Whether the predicted 10x cost reduction materializes given the supply chain constraints Qiao acknowledges, especially for energy and advanced chips.

This decision tree encapsulates the strategic logic companies will face as AI adoption matures, and where Fireworks positions itself—as the enabler of the "fine-tune and deploy" path.
